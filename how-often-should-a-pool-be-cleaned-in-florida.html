How Often Should a Pool Be Cleaned in Florida?

The short answer is once a week, and that holds for almost every residential pool in Florida whether it gets swum in daily or sits untouched for a month. That surprises people who assume a pool nobody uses should need less attention. It does not really work that way, and understanding why makes the whole schedule make more sense.

A Florida Pool Is Working Against You Year-Round

The thing that drives a pool out of balance is not swimmers. It is the environment. We get intense ultraviolet light for most of the year, and UV destroys free chlorine continuously. On a clear July day in Destin, an unstabilized pool can lose the majority of its chlorine in a matter of hours. That is not a slow decline over a week, it is the same afternoon.

Then there is the rain. Our summer storm pattern drops a lot of water into pools very quickly, and rainwater is acidic and completely unsanitized. It dilutes your chlorine, drags pH and total alkalinity downward, and carries in whatever was sitting on the deck and the surrounding roof. A single strong afternoon storm can move a pool's readings further than a week of normal use.

Add the organic load. Pine needles, oak leaves, acorns, pollen in the spring, sand tracked in from the beach, and along the coast a steady film of salt on everything. All of it lands in the water, and organic debris does two things at once: it consumes chlorine as it breaks down, and it feeds the algae that the chlorine is supposed to be preventing.

What Actually Happens Over One Week

On a properly maintained pool, seven days is roughly the outer edge of what the water can absorb without falling behind. Chlorine drops but does not bottom out. Debris settles but has not had time to stain. Filter pressure creeps up but flow is still good. Everything is a small adjustment.

Stretch that to two weeks and the same pool is in a different situation. Chlorine hits zero somewhere around day nine or ten, and from that point on algae has an open field. Leaves that have been on the bottom for twelve days start leaving marks on plaster that a brush will not take off. The skimmer basket fills, flow drops, and the reduced circulation means whatever chemicals are left are not being distributed properly anyway. Now the visit is not maintenance, it is recovery, and recovery costs more in chemicals and time than the visit you skipped.

The Four Things That Change the Answer

1. Sun exposure and stabilizer

A pool in full sun with no shade needs its cyanuric acid level right or it will burn chlorine off faster than you can add it. Stabilizer is what protects chlorine from UV. Too little and your sanitizer evaporates daily. Too much and the chlorine gets locked up and stops working effectively. It is the single most commonly wrong number we find on pools that owners have been maintaining themselves.

2. Bather load

Swimmers add far more to a pool than most people realize: sunscreen, lotions, sweat, body oils and organics that all consume chlorine directly. A vacation rental with twelve guests for a week is a fundamentally different chemical problem than the same pool sitting empty. This is why rental pools in Destin and Miramar Beach frequently need attention beyond weekly in July, while the identical pool in October is fine on a normal schedule.

3. Trees and what falls off them

A pool under heavy pine or oak canopy, which describes a lot of Fort Walton Beach, Santa Rosa Beach and inland Destin, deals with debris continuously. Pine needles are the worst of it because they sink rather than float, so they slide right past the skimmer, settle on the floor and stain it. Those pools need the bottom netted and vacuumed on every visit and the filter cleaned considerably more often than a pool sitting in the open.

4. Rain and storms

Heavy rain dilutes sanitizer and shifts pH and alkalinity. After a serious storm, a pool that was in perfect shape the day before can need a real correction. This is also why the week after a tropical system moves through is our busiest week of any month.

Does a Pool Need Cleaning in Winter?

Yes, though it can often be lighter. Pools here do not freeze and they do not go dormant. Water temperatures drop enough that algae growth slows considerably, but the trees keep dropping, the rain keeps coming, and the chemistry still drifts. Owners who suspend service entirely from November to March almost always pay for it in April, when the water warms up with a winter's worth of organic material sitting in it and the pool goes green in about four days.

What About Skimming Between Visits?

If you want to run a net across the surface between service visits, it genuinely helps, especially during pollen season or after a windy day. What it does not replace is the rest of the list. Brushing the walls disrupts growth before it becomes visible, the baskets have to be emptied to keep flow up, and the chemistry has to actually be tested rather than eyeballed. Clear water is not proof of balanced water. Plenty of pools that look perfect are running at zero chlorine and are three days from turning.

The Bottom Line

For virtually every pool in Northwest Florida, weekly service is the right interval, and it is weekly year-round rather than seasonally. The pools that give their owners trouble are almost never the ones with bad equipment or the wrong chemicals. They are the ones where the schedule slipped, the corrections got bigger each time, and eventually the pool was in recovery more often than it was simply being maintained.
